# AI usage — final_project.ipynb

We used Claude (Anthropic's Claude Code CLI, Opus model).

On the data-plumbing side, we described the FRED series we wanted (NASDAQ, OECD share-price indices, DGS10, DFII10, Case-Shiller, PALLFNFINDEXM for the asset side, and INDPRO, CPIAUCSL, DFII10, BAA10Y for the factor side) and the AI wrote the parameterized `fred()` helper, the duration-approximation `y_tr()` for bond and TIPS total returns, and the monthly resampling pipeline. We checked each series against the FRED documentation and verified the duration approximation against the textbook formula before accepting it.

For the factor regression and risk decomposition, we specified the model — OLS with HAC standard errors at six-lag Newey-West, factor covariance from sample, asset covariance via Sigma_a = B Sigma_f B' + D — and the AI produced the working code, including the `factor_risk_contrib()` function that splits portfolio variance into per-factor and idiosyncratic components. We reviewed the math against our own derivation before running it. The FBRP optimization objective (sum of squared deviations of factor variance shares from 1/K) is non-convex, so we asked the AI to wire it up with SLSQP plus 40 random Dirichlet starts to escape local minima, using the pension-realistic weight bounds we had agreed on (US equity capped at 40%, real estate at 20%, and so on, with a 2% floor). We picked the bounds; the AI implemented the multi-start solve.

The Public Plans Database integration was the most substantial AI-driven build. When we decided to compare against the realized aggregate of all $5.4T of US public pension capital — not just our hand-constructed CalSTRS approximation — we gave the AI the API documentation from publicplansdata.org and asked it to figure out the schema. The AI probed `ListVariables` and `QDataSet`, identified that the universe-aggregate row is the one where both `ppd_PlanType` and `ppd_FiscalYearType` are null, built the bucket-to-proxy mapping (public equity to US/Intl 60/40, private equity 1:1 to US equity as a leveraged-equity proxy, hedge funds 50/50 to equity/bonds, and so on), and wired the PPD aggregate into the factor decomposition, backtest, metrics table, drawdown panel, and stress-period analysis as a peer benchmark to 60/40 and CalSTRS. We also asked the AI to construct a sanity-check section comparing our model-implied PPD-mapped portfolio returns against the PPD's own reported aggregate one-year returns over each fiscal year; the resulting 0.923 correlation gave us confidence that the proxy mapping was reasonable.

After the AI wrote analysis paragraphs, we had it re-execute the notebook and cross-check every numerical claim against the actual outputs. Several drafts contained plausible-but-wrong numbers (for example, claiming the PPD-mapped portfolio was 55% US equity when it was actually 42%); those were caught in a second pass and corrected so the prose matches the printed tables exactly.

There were also places where AI did not help. The choice of macro factors (Growth, Inflation, Real Rates, Credit), the universe of six asset proxies, the FBRP framing as an alternative to CalSTRS, the bucket map from PPD's eight categories to our six-asset universe, and the economic interpretation of every result — why bond-heavy is the right risk-parity outcome, why 2022 is the cleanest validation episode, why drawdown reduction matters more than Sharpe to a liability-driven investor — all came from our team's macro-finance background, not from the AI. The conclusion and caveats section, including the honest acknowledgement that the FBRP solve is in-sample and that the Growth and Inflation factor contributions remain small because of our passive-proxy universe, was authored by us and only lightly polished by the AI.
